## Chapter 5: Discussion, Conclusion, and Recommendations

### 5.1 Introduction

Chapter 4 reported the empirical outcome of testing five hypotheses across three designed experiments, one supplementary investigation, one advisory-feature evaluation, and one external literature comparison. This chapter steps back from those individual results to interpret what they mean together, assess how far the research objectives set out in Section 1.3 were actually met, state the limitations of the study honestly, and set out what should be done next — both by an institution considering deployment and by any researcher extending this work. Where a finding reflects well on the system, that is reported; where a hypothesis was not supported, that is reported with equal weight, since the value of an empirical evaluation lies precisely in its willingness to report an unfavourable result as clearly as a favourable one.

### 5.2 Summary of Findings

Table 5.1 restates the five research objectives from Section 1.3.2 against their empirical outcome, drawing directly on Table 4.8.

**Table 5.1: Research objectives against empirical outcome.**

This is addressed directly in the discussion below rather than reframed away — the purpose of stating a falsifiable, numeric target in Chapter 1 was precisely so that a shortfall would be visible rather than absorbed into qualitative language after the fact.

### 5.3 Discussion of Findings

#### 5.3.1 The Escalation Rate Is the Single Thread Connecting the Two Unmet Targets

Hypothesis 1's target (≥ 80% Tier 1 resolution) and the cost half of Hypothesis 2's target (≤ 30% of cloud-only cost) both failed for the same underlying reason: the corpus, evaluated under this system's precision constraint, requires escalating roughly 47% of submissions to Tier 2, not 20%. This is not two independent shortfalls; it is one measured quantity — the escalation rate — expressed through two different targets set in Chapter 1.

The 80/20 split named in RO2 was set as a design aspiration before any submission had been run through the pipeline, not derived from a pilot measurement on this corpus. In hindsight, the assumption embedded in that target is that the *majority* of plagiarism-detection cases are unambiguous — either clean or blatant copies — with only a minority falling into the genuinely hard, paraphrase-detection middle ground the semantic tier exists to handle. What Experiment 1 (§4.3) actually measured is that, once the escalation band (τ_low, τ_high) is tuned to hold overall precision at 1.000 — a constraint chosen deliberately in Section 3.10.3 because a false plagiarism accusation was judged more costly than a missed one — the resulting band captures a much larger share of the corpus than the aspirational figure assumed. Widening the resolved band to reach 80% would have required narrowing the escalation zone, which was tested and found to violate the precision constraint (§3.10.3, threshold-sensitivity sweep). The 80% target and the 1.000-precision constraint cannot both hold on this corpus; the system was tuned to keep the constraint the institution actually cares about (no wrongful accusations) rather than the target that was easier to hit on paper.

This is corroborated, in general terms, by adaptive-retrieval literature external to this thesis. Arabzadeh, Yan, and Clarke (2021), evaluating classifier-driven routing between sparse and dense retrieval on the MS MARCO benchmark, found that a 50% selective-escalation budget achieved a recall of 0.96 against 0.91 for naive random allocation at the same budget — independent evidence that selectively escalating roughly half a workload, rather than a small minority of it, is where a gated sparse/dense pipeline can meaningfully outperform naive allocation of the same resource. This supports the *general* claim that a near-50% escalation regime is a defensible and productive operating point for this class of system; it does not establish, and this thesis does not claim, that 45–50% is some universal equilibrium point that any sparse/dense system bridging these representations under a comparable precision constraint would necessarily converge to. The claim made here is narrower and fully supported by the data actually available: on this corpus, under this precision constraint, roughly half the workload needs the expensive tier, and that is a real, useful, and reportable number in its own right — arguably more useful to an adopting institution than the 80% aspiration would have been, precisely because it is measured rather than assumed.

#### 5.3.2 Effectiveness Fully Achieved; Cost Substantially, but Not Fully, Reduced

Hypothesis 3 (F1 ≥ 0.84) was clearly supported: at 0.884, the dual-tier configuration outperformed all three baselines tested, including cloud-only (Experiment 2, §4.4). The system does not trade accuracy for its cost savings — it matches or slightly exceeds the accuracy of the more expensive always-escalate configuration, because the local Winnowing signal on Tier 1 resolves the unambiguous majority with a hard containment guarantee that a purely dense retriever has no equivalent for, while nothing about escalating everything to Tier 2 improves on that guarantee for the cases Tier 1 already resolves correctly.

The cost result should be read in the same spirit. A 52.6% reduction relative to cloud-only, even though it falls short of the 70% target implied by Hypothesis 2, is still a substantial, practically meaningful saving for a resource-constrained institution — it is not a marginal result simply because it missed a number chosen in advance. The honest framing is: this system delivers cloud-only-or-better accuracy at roughly half the cost of a cloud-only deployment, not at 30% of it. An institution evaluating this system for adoption should budget accordingly rather than on the basis of the original target.

#### 5.3.3 Component Latency Is Fast; End-to-End Latency Reveals the Real Bottleneck

The gap between the 247ms component-level latency (the detection pipeline itself, once invoked) and the roughly 20-second end-to-end latency (from Moodle submission to a result the instructor can see) is not a contradiction — it is a direct, honest measurement of where time is actually spent in a production-shaped system. The detection algorithm is fast; the asynchronous Celery/Redis task queue between submission and detection, plus whatever polling or refresh interval governs how quickly a result becomes visible in the Moodle interface, accounts for essentially all of the remaining ~19.75 seconds. Hypothesis 4's literal target (end-to-end p95 < 5s) was written without this queuing and UI-refresh overhead accounted for, and was not met as stated.

This distinction matters for how the result should be presented and acted on. Reporting only the 247ms figure would overstate what a user of the deployed system actually experiences; reporting only the ~20s figure would understate how fast the detection logic itself is and would misdirect any future optimisation effort toward the wrong component. The correct next step, addressed in Section 5.7 below, is to treat the ~20-second figure as the real target for improvement and to look specifically at queue dispatch latency and UI refresh cadence — not at the detection algorithm, which is not the bottleneck.

#### 5.3.4 Real-Time Cross-Submission Detection Succeeded in Substance

Hypothesis 5's qualitative real-time demonstration — submitting a paraphrased copy 15 minutes after an original and confirming the system flags the match and surfaces it on the instructor dashboard — succeeded in every trial, within roughly the same ~20-second window discussed above. The literal "supported/not supported" framing borrowed from Hypothesis 4 is a poor fit here: the actual claim under test (that cross-submission detection can operate at submission time, against a corpus that is still being built as students submit, without a separate batch-comparison step) was demonstrated correctly and repeatedly. This thesis reports it as supported in substance, with the caveat that "real-time" should be understood throughout as "within about 20 seconds of submission," not "instantaneous."

#### 5.3.5 Methodological Integrity as a Finding, Not Just a Process Note

Table 4.1's ten defects and the failure-mode episode in Section 4.8 are, on their own, a substantive empirical finding about how this class of result is produced, not merely a changelog. Four of the ten defects (data leakage, the Jaccard/containment gating error, evaluation-harness fingerprint indexing, and threshold-sweep self-contamination) would each independently have produced a plausible-looking but wrong headline number had they gone uncaught — an inflated accuracy from evaluating submissions against copies of themselves, a collapsed similarity score for exactly the short-excerpt-in-long-source case the system is meant to catch, a Tier 1 blind to its own corpus, and a threshold sweep that degenerated into predicting everyone positive. None of these failure modes would have been visible from reading the code in isolation; each was caught only by running the system against real data and checking the result against an independent expectation.

The Section 4.8 episode is the clearest illustration of why that discipline matters even after a result is obtained. The first round of false-positive analysis produced a coherent, literature-consistent explanation — that short answers were saturating the containment score on shared domain vocabulary — and that explanation was wrong. It was only shown to be wrong by tracing each flagged case back to the actual document the system had matched against, which turned out to be the leaked duplicate from defect 1, not the reference source the original analysis had reasoned about by hand. A plausible explanation checked only against itself, rather than against the system's actual behaviour, survived one full round of analysis before being overturned. The general lesson — that an explanation's plausibility is not evidence of its correctness, and that only tracing the system's actual output settles the question — recurs across at least three of the ten logged defects (1, 3, and 8 in Table 4.1, each of which had a first fix attempt that addressed the wrong thing and was only caught by re-running the same live check that had motivated the fix) and is treated here as a general property of this kind of empirical software evaluation, not a one-off embarrassment.

#### 5.3.6 AI-Authorship Detection Is Advisory by Necessity, Not by Conservative Design Choice Alone

The AI-authorship classifier's measured precision (88.2%) and recall (16.7%), with recall collapsing to 6.9% on GPT-4-generated text against 29.4% on GPT-3-generated text, is not a marginal or borderline result — it is a decisive demonstration that this component, as currently built, cannot function as a comprehensive detector. It is trustworthy when it does fire (few false accusations against genuinely human-authored work: 2.1% of the human-authored corpus), but it is wrong far more often than it is right about which AI-generated submissions actually exist, and it gets worse, not better, on the newest generation of models most likely to be encountered in practice going forward.

This finding is independently corroborated by, not merely consistent with, the wider published literature: Weber-Wulff et al.'s (2023) fourteen-tool benchmark found a comparable pattern (74% accuracy on raw AI-generated text collapsing to 42% once the text was lightly edited, with a documented bias toward classifying content as human-written), and Sadasivan et al.'s (2024) theoretical analysis explains why this class of result should be expected: detector reliability is bounded by the statistical distance between the human and machine text distributions the detector was trained to separate, and that distance shrinks as generation quality improves or as a paraphrasing step is introduced. The system's advisory-only governance model — a separate database table, a visually distinct badge, an explicit disclaimer, and no path from this signal into any automated disciplinary action — was adopted on literature grounds before this evaluation and is now independently confirmed by this thesis's own measurement, not merely restated by it.

#### 5.3.7 External Validation Supports the General Architecture, Not Every Specific Number

Section 4.10's comparison against Arabzadeh et al. (2021), Weber-Wulff et al. (2023), Foltýnek et al. (2020), and Reducindo et al. (2017) is deliberately narrower in what it claims than an earlier draft of that section attempted. Two candidate LMS-integration citations were investigated and excluded after they could not be independently verified against their claimed source, rather than retained on the assumption that a plausible-looking reference is a real one. What survives verification is still useful: the general shape of this thesis's dual-tier and advisory-AI-detection findings is independently supported by published literature in both the retrieval and AI-detection domains, even where a specific numeric coincidence between this thesis's results and another paper's results should not be read as more than a general pattern holding in both places.

### 5.4 Contributions to Knowledge

This thesis contributes an empirically measured, rather than assumed, escalation rate for a precision-constrained dual-tier plagiarism-detection pipeline deployed against a real LMS integration, together with the specific finding that this rate (~47%) is substantially higher than a commonly assumed 80/20 split once a zero-tolerance false-positive constraint is actually enforced rather than merely stated as a design goal. It contributes a working, deployed reference architecture — Winnowing containment gated against a capped BM25 signal, escalating to a FAISS/BM25 hybrid — integrated live into Moodle with submission-time cross-submission detection, rather than a retrieval-benchmark result evaluated offline against a static corpus. It contributes an independently reproducible, open-source evaluation harness that produced eight separate evaluation reports, each re-runnable against the same corpus. It contributes a documented case study of methodological-integrity practice in applied systems evaluation: ten specific, named defects, three of which survived an initial incorrect fix, caught only because every claimed result was checked against live behaviour rather than trusted once produced — a discipline this thesis argues should be treated as a reportable methodological finding, not backstage process. Finally, it contributes an empirically validated, rather than merely literature-motivated, governance model for AI-authorship detection as an advisory-only signal in an academic-integrity context, with the specific precision/recall/cross-generator profile that justifies that governance choice measured directly rather than assumed from prior work alone.

### 5.5 Limitations of the Study

Several limitations qualify how far these findings generalise beyond this specific implementation and corpus.

**Corpus scale and composition.** The evaluation corpus comprises 200 labelled submissions across four plagiarism categories, all in English, drawn from an academic-writing context. This is sufficient to support the statistical comparisons reported in Chapter 4 (bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, paired significance testing) but is not large enough, nor drawn from enough institutions, disciplines, or languages, to establish that the measured ~47% escalation rate or the 0.884 F1 score would hold outside this specific corpus's stylistic distribution. Any institution adopting this architecture should expect to re-measure its own escalation rate rather than assume this thesis's figure transfers directly.

**A specification/analysis discrepancy in the test-split size.** Section 3.10 specifies a held-out test split of 40 submissions; the cost-accuracy comparison analysed in Section 4.10.1 (Table 4.5) reports results against a test split of n = 38. This inconsistency was not resolved during this evaluation and should be reconciled — by confirming which figure is correct and, if two submissions were excluded, documenting why — before the corresponding tables are treated as final.

**Threshold tuning was performed on a validation split drawn from the same corpus.** The escalation thresholds (τ_low = 0.30, τ_high = 0.68) that produced the ~47% escalation rate were tuned to satisfy the precision constraint on this corpus's own validation split. This is standard practice, but it means the specific threshold values, and quite possibly the resulting escalation rate itself, are properties of this corpus's particular similarity-score distribution and are not guaranteed to reproduce on a corpus with different length, register, or subject-matter characteristics without re-tuning.

**The AI-authorship evaluation used a single, lightweight classifier.** The 88.2%/16.7% precision/recall profile characterises `Hello-SimpleAI/chatgpt-detector-roberta` specifically, chosen for its ability to run on CPU within a modest memory footprint. More accurate published alternatives exist (Binoculars, in particular) but require two large language models resident in memory simultaneously and were not empirically evaluated against this corpus, since they fall outside the deployment hardware constraint this thesis targeted. The AI-detection findings in this thesis should therefore be read as a characterisation of one deployable, resource-constrained classifier, not as an upper bound on what AI-authorship detection can achieve in general.

**Data leakage was found and corrected, not absent by design.** Defect 1 in Table 4.1 — every Clough & Stevenson submission also present, under a different filename, in the source-matching corpus — was caught during this evaluation rather than prevented by the corpus construction process itself. Its correction is reflected in every result reported in Chapter 4, but its initial presence is a caution about corpus hygiene generally: a similarly structured but undetected leakage elsewhere in the corpus cannot be ruled out with certainty, only judged less likely given the scrutiny already applied.

**The end-to-end latency figure was measured in a single deployment environment.** The ~20-second end-to-end latency reflects this system's specific Celery/Redis configuration and Moodle polling interval on its evaluation deployment; it is a property of that configuration, not an inherent property of the dual-tier detection approach, and could plausibly be reduced substantially by configuration changes not investigated in this thesis (Section 5.7).

### 5.6 Conclusion

The research aim stated in Section 1.3.1 was to build a dual-tier plagiarism-detection system that matches cloud-only semantic accuracy while cutting costs substantially through intelligent workload partitioning, with real-time Moodle integration and cross-submission detection at submission time. Judged against that aim directly: accuracy was not merely matched but exceeded (F1 = 0.884 against cloud-only's lower score); cost was cut substantially, by 52.6%, though not to the 30%-of-cloud-only figure originally targeted; a functional, live Moodle integration with submission-time cross-submission detection was delivered and demonstrated working repeatedly; and the entire evaluation was produced through an open, reproducible harness rather than a one-off measurement.

Two specific numeric targets set in Chapter 1 — the 80% Tier 1 resolution rate and the 30%-of-cloud-only cost ceiling — were not met, and this thesis reports that plainly rather than reframe the targets after the fact. Both shortfalls trace to the same measured cause: this corpus, under a precision constraint the system was deliberately tuned to satisfy rather than relax, requires escalating roughly 47% of submissions to its more expensive tier. The research aim is substantially achieved; two of its five formally stated hypotheses are not, for a specific, understood, and defensible reason rather than an unexplained one. In the framing of design-science research evaluation (Hevner et al., 2004), an artefact evaluated honestly against numeric criteria set in advance — including criteria it fails to meet — is a stronger contribution than one whose criteria are quietly adjusted to match whatever was achieved, and this thesis has aimed throughout to be the former.

### 5.7 Recommendations

For an institution evaluating this architecture for adoption, four practical recommendations follow directly from the findings above:

1. **Budget for a roughly 50% Tier 2 escalation rate, not 20%**, if a zero-tolerance false-positive policy is to be enforced. Relaxing the precision constraint slightly would likely reduce the escalation rate and cost further, at the price of accepting some non-zero false-positive rate; this trade-off should be a deliberate institutional policy decision, not a parameter left at its evaluation-time default.
2. **Treat the AI-authorship signal as a prompt for a human conversation, never as evidence on its own.** Its own measured recall means the large majority of genuinely AI-generated submissions will not be flagged, and any use of it as a primary detection mechanism, rather than the advisory signal it is designed and validated to be, would be a direct misuse of a measured result this thesis reports specifically to prevent.
3. **Plan the user-facing turnaround expectation around ~20 seconds, not the 247ms component figure.** Setting instructor and student expectations around the wrong number would be a direct and avoidable consequence of citing this thesis's fastest, rather than its most representative, latency figure.
4. **Re-run the evaluation harness against the adopting institution's own submission corpus before trusting this thesis's specific escalation rate, F1 score, or cost figures.** Every headline number in Chapter 4 is a property of this specific 200-submission corpus; Section 5.5 above details why none of them should be assumed to transfer unmeasured.

### 5.8 Suggestions for Future Work

Six directions follow directly from the limitations and findings discussed above, rather than being offered as generic extensions:

1. **Investigate the precision/escalation-rate trade-off directly.** A systematic sweep of the precision constraint itself (not just the thresholds that satisfy a fixed precision target, as Section 3.10.3 already did) would establish how much the escalation rate — and therefore cost — could be reduced for each increment of false-positive risk accepted, giving an adopting institution an actual trade-off curve rather than a single operating point.
2. **Close the end-to-end/component latency gap.** Since Section 5.3.3 identified the Celery/Redis queue dispatch and Moodle UI refresh cadence, not the detection algorithm, as the source of the ~19.75-second overhead, targeted profiling of those two specific components — rather than further optimisation of the detection pipeline itself — is the direction most likely to yield a real reduction in perceived turnaround time.
3. **Reconcile and re-verify the test-split size discrepancy (40 vs. 38 submissions)** identified in Section 5.5, and re-run the affected comparisons on the corrected split before any figures from Table 4.5 are cited outside this thesis.
4. **Expand corpus scale, discipline coverage, and language coverage**, and re-measure escalation rate, F1, and cost on each, to establish which of this thesis's figures are properties of the dual-tier architecture generally and which are properties of this specific English-language, single-institution corpus.
5. **Empirically evaluate a stronger AI-authorship detector (e.g., Binoculars or Fast-DetectGPT) against the same 185-item labelled corpus**, once the deployment-hardware constraint that ruled them out in this thesis is reconsidered, to establish whether the precision/recall asymmetry measured here is a property of AI-text detection generally or specifically of the lightweight classifier this thesis was constrained to deploy.
6. **Conduct a formal usability evaluation against Foltýnek et al.'s (2020) twenty-plus-criterion rubric**, applying it directly to this system rather than only comparing this system's general design motivation to their published conclusions, as an already-identified but not yet executed piece of future work (§4.10.3).

### 5.9 Chapter Summary

This chapter interpreted Chapter 4's results against the five research objectives set out in Chapter 1, finding the core research aim substantially achieved — accuracy matched and exceeded, cost substantially though not fully reduced, real-time LMS integration delivered and demonstrated, and a reproducible evaluation framework produced — while reporting plainly that two specific numeric hypotheses (Tier 1 resolution rate and the cost-reduction ceiling) were not met, for a single, well-understood, measured reason: a precision-constrained escalation rate of roughly 47%, not the 20% originally assumed. It identified the discipline of checking every claimed result against live system behaviour, evidenced by ten logged defects and one overturned failure-mode explanation, as a substantive finding about how this class of result should be produced, not merely a process note. It set out the study's limitations honestly, including a corpus-scale limitation, an unresolved test-split discrepancy, and the AI-detection evaluation's scope being limited to a single lightweight classifier. It closed with concrete recommendations for institutional adopters and six specific directions for future work, each following directly from a finding or limitation established earlier in this chapter.
